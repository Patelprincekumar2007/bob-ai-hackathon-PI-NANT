# Problem Statement

## Background

Global supply chains in 2025 are under unprecedented pressure. Geopolitical instability, climate change, and post-pandemic congestion have created an environment where disruptions are no longer exceptional — they are routine. Container shipping handles approximately 90% of world trade, and even a single day of delay at a major hub can cascade across dozens of shipments, carriers, and consignees. Supply chain operators are expected to manage this complexity in real time, often with outdated tools built for a more predictable era.

## The Problem

Logistics coordinators managing 20 to 200 active shipments face four categories of disruption that cause the most damage:

1. **Port congestion** — A major port such as Shanghai (CNSHA) or Rotterdam (NLRTM) can accumulate multi-day backlogs within hours of a weather event, labour dispute, or sudden surge in vessel arrivals. Cargo waits on vessels, demurrage costs accrue, and downstream delivery commitments break.

2. **Extreme weather** — Cyclones, winter storms, and fog closures force vessels to divert or anchor. Temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical and fresh-produce cargo is at heightened risk because a delay of even 24–48 hours outside a refrigerated environment can render it unsalvageable.

3. **Labour strikes** — Port and rail strikes — increasingly common across Europe and North America — shut down entire trade corridors with little advance warning. The Port of Rotterdam strike in 2024, for example, displaced over 60 vessel calls in a single week.

4. **Vessel failures** — Mechanical breakdowns and dry-docking force emergency cargo transhipment. Coordinating replacement vessels across multiple carriers manually takes 4–8 hours of back-and-forth email and phone calls.

## Who Is Affected

The primary affected user is the **logistics coordinator** — the operations professional who monitors a shipment board of 20–200 active consignments and is responsible for delivery timelines, carrier relations, and SLA compliance. Secondary affected parties include:

- **Operations managers** who escalate when CRITICAL shipments miss contractual delivery windows
- **Consignees** (factories, hospitals, retailers) whose production schedules and stock levels depend on on-time delivery
- **Finance teams** who absorb demurrage, re-routing, and SLA penalty costs

## Why It Matters

The cost of supply chain disruptions is measurable and large:

- Average cost of a major supply chain disruption: **$100M+** per incident for large shippers (McKinsey, 2023)
- Cold-chain failures alone cause an estimated **$35B** of pharmaceutical product loss annually
- SLA breach penalties in pharmaceutical logistics can reach **$50,000 per day**
- Manual rerouting consumes **4–8 hours** of coordinator time per incident — time spent on carrier portals, spreadsheets, and email chains rather than strategic decisions

## Why Existing Solutions Fall Short

Current tools fail the logistics coordinator in three critical ways:

| Gap | Description |
|---|---|
| **Siloed visibility** | Carrier tracking portals show status for one carrier at a time. There is no unified risk view across all active shipments. |
| **No AI prioritisation** | Coordinators must manually decide which disrupted shipments are most urgent. There is no automated scoring that weighs delay, deadline pressure, cargo type, and disruption severity together. |
| **No integrated recommendations** | Knowing a shipment is disrupted is only half the problem. Existing tools do not suggest concrete alternative routes, carriers, or vehicles — coordinators must research these manually. |
| **Cold-chain blindness** | Temperature monitoring data, if available at all, lives in a separate IoT platform disconnected from the logistics management system. |
| **No natural-language interface** | Coordinators cannot ask "which shipments need my attention right now?" in plain English — they must navigate multiple systems and apply their own judgment. |
